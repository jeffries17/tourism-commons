Chapter 1

Welcome to the Toolkit

You are already an experienced bird guide. The purpose of this toolkit is not to teach you how to guide — it is to help you communicate your expertise to an international audience.

Why This Toolkit?

The Bird Watchers' Association, The Gambia has earned a strong reputation through the knowledge, professionalism and hospitality of its licensed bird guides. International trade events such as Global Birdfair provide an opportunity to share that expertise with specialist tour operators, bird clubs, travel media and conservation organisations from around the world.

Guiding visitors and meeting international buyers are different skills, but they are built on the same foundation: knowledge, trust and good communication. This toolkit has been developed to help participants present their experience confidently, communicate effectively and build long-term professional relationships.

Although prepared for Global Birdfair 2026, the guidance is intended to remain useful for future trade fairs, online meetings, familiarisation visits and conversations with international tourism partners.

What Does Success Look Like?

A successful trade fair is not measured by how many brochures you hand out or business cards you collect. Instead, success comes from starting conversations that continue after the event.

One good meeting may lead to:

  • a follow-up online meeting;
  • a request for an itinerary or quotation;
  • inclusion in a future tour programme;
  • or the beginning of a long-term partnership.

Focus on building trust rather than trying to secure immediate business.

Key Message

International buyers are looking for knowledgeable local partners they can trust — not perfect sales presentations.

Before You Move On

Remember

Your knowledge and experience are your greatest strengths. This toolkit will help you present them with confidence.

Try This

Introduce yourself in 30 seconds without mentioning a single bird species. Explain who you are, your experience as a guide, and what makes birdwatching in The Gambia special.

Chapter 2

Understanding Your Future Partners

Recognising who you are speaking with will help you ask better questions, share more relevant information and have more productive conversations.

As part of this project, a wide range of specialist birdwatching tour operators, wildlife travel companies, conservation organisations and industry partners were reviewed to identify potential opportunities for the Bird Watchers' Association.

Already selling The Gambia

Naturetrek, Birdfinders, Heatherlea, Rockjumper, Oriole Birding, Limosa, Wildlife Worldwide. Focus on how you can add value to what they already offer.

Exploring new destinations

Birds Wildlife & Nature Tours, Birder.Travel, 2by2 Holidays. Your objective is to introduce the destination and explore whether it complements what they already offer.

Regional & conservation partners

African Bird Club, Fauna & Flora. These meetings focus on conservation, guide development and shared projects — not tour prices.

Asking Better Questions

Instead of immediately describing your services, consider opening with questions such as:

  • "What types of birdwatching tours do your clients enjoy most?"
  • "Are you looking to expand into new destinations?"
  • "What qualities do you value in a local guiding partner?"
  • "What challenges have you experienced when operating birdwatching tours?"
  • "How can we best support your programmes in The Gambia?"

Listening carefully often provides more valuable information than delivering a long presentation.

Guide's Tip

When meeting an existing Gambia operator, avoid spending the entire meeting explaining the destination. Instead, ask what they are looking for and how you can help improve their programmes.

Example
Meeting with Naturetrek

Rather than explaining why The Gambia is a good birdwatching destination, ask how demand has changed in recent years and what opportunities they see for new itineraries or specialist departures. This shifts the conversation from promotion to partnership.

Meeting with African Bird Club

Focus on conservation, guide development and opportunities to promote Gambian birdwatching through shared projects and member engagement, rather than discussing tour prices or logistics.

Key Message

The best B2B conversations begin with understanding your partner's needs before explaining your own services.

Before You Move On

Remember

Different organisations attend trade events for different reasons. The more you understand their objectives, the easier it becomes to identify opportunities for collaboration.

Chapter 3

Telling the Gambian Birding Story

The Story in One Page

The Gambian birding story is not mainly about remoteness or rarity. It is a story about concentration, accessibility, and confidence. The country is small — about 11,300 km², roughly 320 km long and only 50 km wide at its broadest — yet it combines Atlantic coast, estuary, mangroves, lagoons, mudflats, gallery forest, semi-evergreen forest, rice fields, savanna woodland, and the River Gambia corridor in a very compact area.

English is the official language, the country has an established tourism base, and it is around six hours from major European gateways. That matters in B2B conversations: clients can arrive for a short winter-sun break and still experience serious birding, or add upriver nights for more specialist targets.

Core Message

The Gambia offers a high-density West African birding experience in a destination that is compact, English-speaking, and straightforward to operate.

What Birders Come For

The African Bird Club cites approximately 580 recorded species; broader checklists such as Avibase list 629 species. The destination is not primarily sold as an endemic-bird destination. Rather, it is sold as an accessible concentration of West African and biome-restricted birds, mixed with strong numbers of wintering Palearctic migrants.

Key birds partners mention: Blue-bellied Roller, Ahanta Francolin, Western Bluebill, Green Turaco, Bearded Barbet, White-fronted Black-chat, Egyptian Plover, African Finfoot.

Guide's Tip

Do not lead with the species total alone. Lead with what that total means: "a very bird-rich country where clients can experience coast, mangroves, forest, wetlands, and savanna in one trip without difficult logistics."

Habitats & Signature Sites

Coastal lagoons, mudflats & offshore islands. Tanji/Bijol, Niumi, and the Tanbi-Bund Road area. The Bijol Islands are the only known seabird breeding site in The Gambia and can hold more than 20,000 waterbirds.

Mangroves, tidal creeks & estuarine wetlands. Tanbi and Bao Bolong — the country's most diverse inland wetland, with regular concentrations of 20,000+ waterbirds between August and December.

Forest and woodland. Abuko Nature Reserve — probably the most intensively birdwatched forest in West Africa, with 270+ species recorded. Pirang and Kiang West add forest fragments and open savanna specialties including Blue-bellied Roller.

Upriver and riverine extensions. Tendaba, Georgetown/Janjangbureh, and river sites for Egyptian Plover and African Finfoot. This is the country's strongest card for specialist and photography-focused departures.

When to Go

MonthsBirding highlights
Oct–NovPost-wet-season greenery; weavers and bishops still in breeding plumage; first migrants arriving. Excellent photography.
Dec–FebCore dry season. Migrants fully present. Easiest logistics and strongest mainstream visitor comfort.
Mar–AprHotter and drier; grasses reduced, better visibility in some habitats; good upriver access continues.
May–SepRain builds and peaks. More challenging for standard tours, though locally interesting for breeding activity and lush habitats.

Adapting Your Pitch by Partner Type

Specialist birding operators: emphasize species density, habitat variety in short transfers, reliable local guiding, and the option to combine a simple coastal base with upriver specialities.

General wildlife operators: emphasize winter-sun accessibility, English, short flying time, birds plus primates/wetlands/river experiences, and the possibility of photography-friendly departures.

Bird clubs: emphasize safety, ease, flat terrain, manageable group logistics, and the fact that a mixed-ability group can still see a very rewarding range of African birds in a week.

Conservation partners: emphasize wetlands, mangroves, Ramsar and KBA importance, the link between birdwatching and livelihoods for licensed local guides, and opportunities for training, monitoring and community outreach.

For a specialist birding operator

"The Gambia is one of the easiest ways to introduce clients to West African birding. In a compact, English-speaking destination you can cover coastal wetlands, mangroves, forest, savanna, and upriver sites, with around 580-plus regularly cited species and a strong mix of West African specialities and Palearctic migrants. For shorter departures, the coast works well; for specialist groups, upriver extensions add birds like Egyptian Plover and African Finfoot."

For a general wildlife operator

"The Gambia works very well as a soft-adventure nature destination: six hours from Europe, established tourism infrastructure, English widely used, and excellent winter climate. Birding can be combined with river trips, mangroves, forest lodges, primates, and culture, which makes it attractive even for clients who are not full-time birders."

For a bird club or society

"The Gambia is ideal for clubs because it offers a real African birding experience without difficult travel. Many sites are accessible from the coast, walking is usually light to moderate, and members of different experience levels can enjoy colourful, visible birds, waterbirds, raptors, and photographers' species in a relatively short programme."

For a conservation partner

"The Gambia's story is not only tourism. Its mangroves, estuaries, coastal islands, and inland wetlands are internationally important for waterbirds and migrants, with several Ramsar and KBA sites already identified. Local bird guiding can be part of conservation delivery through training, visitor education, monitoring, and community-based stewardship."

Chapter 4

Presenting Yourself Professionally

"International buyers are meeting dozens of operators over two or three days. They may only remember a handful. A clear introduction, confident communication, and genuine enthusiasm often leave a stronger impression than a long sales pitch."

Why First Impressions Matter

The first minute of a meeting often shapes everything that follows. Buyers are quietly asking themselves:

  • Is this guide professional?
  • Do they understand international visitors?
  • Can I trust them with my clients?
  • Are they easy to communicate with?
  • Do they genuinely know their destination?

You do not need to sound like a salesperson. The goal is to sound knowledgeable, organised and enthusiastic.

Your 30-Second Introduction

At Birdfair you will often have only 30 seconds before someone decides whether to continue the conversation. Aim to answer four questions:

1
Who are you? Your name and background.
2
Who do you work with? The BWA or your own company.
3
What do you offer? Your guiding services and destination.
4
Why is it interesting? One compelling reason to keep talking.
Example

"Hello, I'm Pa from the Bird Watchers' Association, The Gambia. We represent licensed bird guides across the country and organise tailor-made birdwatching experiences for international visitors. The Gambia is one of Africa's easiest birding destinations, with over 550 bird species in a compact country, and we work closely with overseas tour operators to create memorable birding holidays."

Explaining the Bird Watchers' Association

Many buyers will never have heard of the Association. Do not assume they understand how it works. A simple explanation:

"The Bird Watchers' Association is the professional organisation representing licensed bird guides in The Gambia. We promote professional guiding standards, support conservation, and help connect international visitors and tour operators with experienced local guides across the country."

You might also explain that guides are licensed, members work throughout the country, and enquiries can be directed through the Association.

Explaining Your Own Experience

Avoid simply saying "I've been guiding for many years." Instead, be specific. You might mention:

  • years guiding and favourite locations;
  • specialist birds or habitats you know best;
  • photography experience;
  • languages spoken;
  • conservation activities;
  • experience with international visitors and which nationalities.
Example

"I've specialised in bird guiding for more than fifteen years. Much of my work is with visitors from the UK, Germany and the Netherlands, and I particularly enjoy helping photographers and keen birders find species such as Egyptian Plover, Abyssinian Ground Hornbill and White-backed Night Heron."

Professional Communication Tips

  • Listen more than you speak.
  • Answer questions directly.
  • Speak slowly and clearly.
  • If you do not know an answer, say you will find out.
  • Avoid exaggerating species sightings or guarantees.
  • Smile and show genuine enthusiasm.
  • Be curious about the buyer's business as well as talking about your own.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

✗Speaking for ten minutes without asking a question.
✗Listing every bird species you know.
✗Beginning immediately with prices.
✗Criticising other guides or operators.
✗Using complicated tourism jargon.
✗Trying to memorise a script word-for-word.
Remember

International buyers are looking for reliable long-term partners, not perfect presentations. Be yourself. Be professional. Be enthusiastic. Be prepared.

Before You Move On

Practice Exercise

Write your own personal 30-second introduction. Use the four-question structure above. Try saying it aloud and time yourself — can you say it comfortably in under 30 seconds?

Chapter 5

Presenting Your Birding Experiences

"International buyers are not simply looking for itineraries — they are looking for experiences that will appeal to their clients and fit within their existing programmes."

Describing Your Tours

When describing your tours, avoid listing activities one after another. Instead, help buyers understand the overall experience and who it is designed for. A strong birdwatching product should answer six simple questions:

  1. What is the experience?
  2. Who is it designed for?
  3. How long does it last?
  4. What makes it unique?
  5. What is included?
  6. Why should a visitor choose it?

Remember that buyers are thinking about their own clients. The easier it is for them to imagine the experience, the easier it becomes to include it in their programmes.

Matching Experiences to Different Travellers

Half-day and full-day excursions are ideal for visitors with limited time or those adding birdwatching to a wider holiday.

Multi-day birding tours allow visitors to experience a greater variety of habitats and target species while exploring different regions of The Gambia.

Photography tours often move at a slower pace, placing greater emphasis on lighting, positioning and time spent observing individual species.

Tailor-made itineraries allow you to adapt the experience around a visitor's interests, travel dates, pace and birding goals. Flexibility is often one of the Association's greatest strengths.

Guide's Tip

Rather than saying "We offer half-day and full-day tours," explain who each experience is designed for and why a visitor might choose it.

Talking About Prices

Price is important, but it should rarely be the first topic of conversation. Instead of simply quoting a price, explain what the visitor receives:

  • professional guiding;
  • transport;
  • park or reserve entrance fees (where applicable);
  • meals or refreshments;
  • drinking water;
  • accommodation (for multi-day tours);
  • flexibility to adapt the itinerary.

Being clear about what is included helps buyers compare experiences fairly and reduces misunderstandings later.

Key Message

Present experiences, not just itineraries. Help buyers understand who each tour is for and why it will appeal to their clients.

Before You Move On

Try This

Choose one of your birdwatching tours and explain it in one minute. Focus on the experience rather than simply describing the route or listing bird species. Answer the six questions above.

Chapter 6

Conducting Successful B2B Meetings

"A successful B2B meeting is not a presentation — it is the beginning of a professional relationship."

Before the Meeting

Good meetings begin before you sit down. Take a few minutes to review the organisation's website and understand the types of tours they offer, the destinations they specialise in, and the clients they serve.

Before the meeting, make sure you have:

  • business cards;
  • a notebook and pen;
  • your itinerary or product sheets;
  • pricing information (if requested); and
  • a phone or tablet with your website available if needed.

Turning a Meeting into a Conversation

Many first-time exhibitors believe they need to spend the entire meeting talking about themselves. In reality, the most successful meetings are balanced conversations.

Start by introducing yourself, then learn about the other organisation. Questions such as:

  • "What type of birdwatching tours do you specialise in?"
  • "Do you already operate in West Africa?"
  • "What do your clients enjoy most?"
  • "What are you looking for in a local partner?"
Guide's Tip

Aim to listen as much as you speak. Buyers are often more interested in discussing their own programmes than listening to a long presentation.

Answering Questions with Confidence

You should be prepared to answer questions about your guiding experience, birdwatching seasons, accommodation and transport, itinerary options, pricing and inclusions, group sizes, and how tours can be customised.

If you do not know an answer, it is perfectly acceptable to say that you will confirm the information after the meeting. Honest, accurate answers build greater confidence than guessing.

Finishing the Meeting Well

Always leave a meeting with a clear understanding of the next step. Before saying goodbye:

  • summarise the main points discussed;
  • agree on any follow-up actions;
  • exchange business cards or contact details; and
  • thank your partner for their time.

As soon as the meeting finishes, make a few notes while the conversation is still fresh. Record who you met, what interested them, and what information you promised to send.

Key Message

The goal of a B2B meeting is not to secure an immediate booking — it is to begin a professional relationship built on trust.

Chapter 7

Preparing Your Marketing Materials

"Your marketing materials should support the conversation — not replace it."

Keep It Simple

International buyers do not expect you to arrive with a suitcase full of brochures. Instead, they want clear, accurate information that is easy to review after the meeting. Focus on quality rather than quantity. A small collection of well-prepared materials is often more effective than dozens of different documents.

Your Essential Toolkit

Before attending a trade fair, make sure you have:

  • Business cards with current contact details.
  • A QR code linking to your website or digital profile.
  • A professional website or online profile.
  • Recent TripAdvisor reviews or other trusted testimonials.
  • High-quality bird and guiding photographs.
  • Sample itineraries.
  • A simple price sheet or pricing guide.
  • WhatsApp and email ready for follow-up communication.
Guide's Tip

Before you travel, ask a colleague to review your website and marketing materials. Fresh eyes often spot broken links, outdated information or missing details.

Digital First

Many buyers prefer digital materials that they can save and share with colleagues after the event. Where possible, provide downloadable itineraries, PDF brochures, links to your website, photo galleries, and social media or TripAdvisor pages. A QR code is often the quickest way to share these resources during a meeting.

Key Message

Good marketing materials support your conversation — they do not replace it.

Before You Move On

Try This

Ask someone unfamiliar with your business to review your website and one itinerary. Can they quickly understand what you offer and how to contact you? Note any changes you need to make.

Chapter 8

Following Up After the Fair

"The meeting starts the relationship. The follow-up helps it grow."

Why Follow-Up Matters

Most partnerships are not created during a trade fair — they develop afterwards. International buyers often meet dozens of organisations over a few days. A thoughtful follow-up helps them remember your conversation and demonstrates that you are organised, reliable and interested in working together.

Aim to send your first follow-up email within one week of returning home.

Writing an Effective Follow-Up

A good follow-up email should be short and personal. Thank the person for their time, remind them where you met, and include any information you promised to send, such as an itinerary, price guide or website link. Where appropriate, invite them to continue the conversation with an online meeting.

Guide's Tip

Personalise every message. Mention something you discussed during the meeting so the recipient immediately remembers your conversation.

Staying Connected

Not every contact will be ready to work together immediately. Stay in touch by connecting on LinkedIn where appropriate, sharing occasional updates or new itineraries, sending relevant news from The Gambia, and responding promptly to future enquiries. Professional relationships often develop over months or even years.

Key Message

Consistent, professional follow-up builds stronger partnerships than a single successful meeting.

Before You Move On

Try This

Draft a follow-up email to someone you met at a trade fair. Keep it to five short paragraphs or fewer and include one clear next step.
