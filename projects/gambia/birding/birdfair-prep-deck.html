Buyer psychology

What a birding specialist cares about

How they plan

Research-first travellers

  • Arrives with a target species list
  • Uses eBird and Merlin before booking
  • Reads field guides and trip reports
  • Books through specialist operators — not Booking.com
  • Often travels in small groups of 6–10
How they judge your value

Expertise over everything else

  • Species per day — not stars per hotel
  • Guide's ornithological depth: "do you know the warblers?"
  • Access to key habitats: Abuko, Kotu, upcountry sites
  • Track record on target species: Egyptian Plover, African Finfoot
  • Professional licensing and BWA affiliation

Key point: a birding buyer already knows what birdwatching is. They're testing whether you know Gambia's birds better than they do. Depth matters more than enthusiasm.

Working the fair floor · Rutland Water · 10–12 July

Three ways to meet buyers

You're not limited to one approach — but know which mode you're in and what to expect from each.

🪑
Mode 1 · Passive

Let them come to you

Visitors approach your stand directly. Usually birding enthusiasts and independent travellers — sometimes travel agents. Good for practice and visibility, but not where most trade business gets done.

Usually tourists and agents — good B2C practice
🚶
Mode 2 · Active

Walk the floor

You go to them. Approach operator stands directly — 5 to 10 minutes, unannounced. Works best when you've researched the operator and have a specific, concise opening. Know who you're approaching before you walk up.

Best for medium-priority operators you didn't pre-book
📅
Mode 3 · Scheduled

Book appointments in advance

A pre-booked 15–20 minute slot means you're in their diary before the floor gets crowded. These are your most valuable conversations — longer, calmer, more focused. This is why the virtual fair matters.

The pitch

Approaching B2B vs B2C

The conversation changes depending on who you're talking to

The key shift

How your presentation changes

B2C — talking to a tourist B2B — talking to an operator
Who is deciding? The person in front of you A professional buying on behalf of 6–15 clients
What they're asking "Will I enjoy this?" "Can I sell this reliably to my clients?"
What you lead with Experience — beauty, discovery, wonder Product — net rate, group size, what's included
What they need to take away Inspiration to book A fact sheet they can price and propose
Decision timeline Days to weeks Months — they plan seasons in advance

Remember: the operator is not the end client. They need to explain Gambia to their customers and mark your price up to cover their costs. Give them the tools to do that — not just a great story.

Two types of trade operator

Adjust based on what they already know

Both columns are trade operators — not tourists. The difference is how much they already know about Gambia.

They already sell Gambia

Don't sell the destination. Sell the guides.

  • Open with: "You know Gambia — we want to make sure you know us."
  • Introduce the BWA team as the professionals behind their product
  • Offer something new: upcountry extension, Senegal crossing, Egyptian Plover site
  • Ask a concrete question: "Are you confirmed for 2026/27? Can we lock in dates?"
  • Leave with a confirmed contact and a follow-up call scheduled
They don't know Gambia yet

Understand why before you pitch.

  • Before you get into specifics: ask why they don't sell Africa — the answer changes everything
  • If they're open to it: lead with the comparison — closer than Kenya, 550+ species, direct UK flights
  • Use a species hook: Egyptian Plover, African Finfoot — not available in East Africa
  • Introduce the structure: BWA guides, Birding Adventures Gambia, Pa Birding Tours
  • Leave a clear leave-behind and propose a call after the fair

Common traps

What Goes Wrong

Six things that cost you the meeting — and how to sidestep them

Six traps to sidestep

Don't lose the room

1

Walking up without knowing why they don't sell Africa. Before you pitch Gambia's specifics, understand the reason. If they haven't done Africa at all, that's a different conversation — and it needs to come first.

2

Talking too generally. "We do great birding in Gambia" tells an operator nothing they can sell. Be specific: species, reliable sites, guide credentials, track record.

3

No leave-behind. By day three of the fair, they've met 80 people. If they can't find your details the next morning, you don't exist. A clear one-page document is essential.

4

Quoting a retail price to an operator. If your price is the same price their client would pay, there's no margin left for them. Give a net rate and let the operator do the markup.

5

Not speaking at a birder's level. These operators know birds. If you stay surface-level — "great variety, beautiful country" — they'll tune out. Speak to the species, the sites, the details that matter to serious birders.

6

No follow-up after the fair. The fair ends on Sunday. If you haven't emailed by Tuesday, you've been forgotten. 48 hours is the window. After that, the chance is mostly gone.

After the fair

After the Fair

Pricing for operators — and the follow-up email

Pricing basics

Two prices, not one

Operators need a net rate — not the price their clients pay.

Your price to the operator

Net rate

This is what you charge. The operator adds their margin on top — typically 25–35% — to create the retail price they sell to clients.

  • Your daily guiding fee
  • Vehicle and transport
  • Site entry fees you arrange
  • Hotel (operator books direct)
  • International flights
  • Client meals
What the client pays

Retail price

The operator sets this. It includes your net rate plus their own costs and margin. Naturetrek charges around £3,500 per person for a Gambia week — your net rate is one part of that total.

Rule: never quote a retail price to an operator. There's no margin left for them, and they won't come back.

Be ready to say: daily guiding rate per person for groups of 6 and groups of 10 · minimum group size · what's included and what's not

The follow-up

Send this within 48 hours

Short, specific, and with one clear next step.

Subject: Following up — [Your name], BWA Gambia · Birdfair

Dear [Name],


Good to meet you at Birdfair on [day]. I'm [your name], a licensed bird guide with the Bird Watchers' Association of The Gambia — [Birding Adventures Gambia / Pa Birding Tours / BWA].


[One sentence about what you discussed — their existing Gambia tour, or the possibility of adding one, or a specific site or species you mentioned.]


I've attached our suggested itinerary and net rate for a standard group departure.


Would you have 20 minutes for a call in the coming weeks?


Best wishes,
[Your name · Company · Phone / Email]

What to attach: your suggested itinerary, net rate for groups of 6 and 10, guide team and BWA credentials, contact details. One or two pages — not a brochure.

Next steps

Before July

These apply whether you're meeting operators who already know Gambia or those you're approaching for the first time.

1

Build a suggested itinerary. One concrete product — a 7 or 8-day birding itinerary you can reference in every conversation and leave behind with buyers. This is what operators will price up and sell.

2

Set your net rate for that itinerary. Know your guiding rate per person for groups of 6 and 10, and your rates for any other itineraries you can offer. Have these numbers ready before any operator meeting.

3

Pre-schedule your top meetings at the virtual fair. Use June to request appointments at Rutland. The earlier you're in a buyer's diary, the more likely the conversation happens.

4

Draft your follow-up email now. Write it before the fair. After Rutland you'll be tired. A prepared email sent on time is worth more than a perfect one sent a week late.
