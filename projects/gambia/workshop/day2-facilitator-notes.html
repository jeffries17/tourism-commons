Slide 1 — What we agreed on Day 1
5–8 min

Reading the commitment board — how to synthesise it live

The slide shows four category chips — Monitor, Make findable, Open a door, Share + coordinate. Your job is to briefly read back what's actually on the board under each category. Block 4 · Track + Coordinate · Slides 4–21 · 9:00–10:30
Slide 4 — Block 4 divider

Entering Block 4

Read the sub-text: "A simple check done consistently is more valuable than a comprehensive study done once every two years." Let it land. This is the conceptual frame for the next 90 minutes: routine over sophistication, consistency over comprehensiveness.

Slide 5 — "Routine beats sophistication."

Let the pull quote do the work

Advance to this slide and pause. Read it aloud. Then read the sub-line: "The goal is awareness, not analysis. Three questions. Monthly. Shared." Then pause again. Don't immediately explain — give the room a moment with the idea.

  • If there's a participant who raised "we don't have the capacity" yesterday, this slide is for them specifically. You can say: "If this is the only thing you take from today — this sentence — the monitoring framework will work."
  • The reason this slide exists as a standalone is that Block 4 will show several tools, some of which look like work. The pull quote pre-emptively reduces that anxiety: you don't need all of them; you need a consistent version of any of them.
Slide 6 — Three monitoring questions

Grounding the framework in questions, not tasks

Read the three questions as if you mean them: "Are we being found by the right audiences? Are the people who visit having the experience we want them to have? Are things getting better or worse over time?" Pause after the third one.

  • Say explicitly: "The third question is why consistency matters. You can't answer 'are things getting better?' if you only look once." The pink accent on "over time" makes this visible on the slide.
  • Ask: "Which of these three questions does your institution most need to be able to answer right now?" Take 2–3 quick responses. Note: different institutions will have different answers, which is itself useful — it shows the monitoring work can be distributed.
Slide 7 — Three tools overview

Naming all three before going into detail

Spend 1–2 minutes on this overview slide. Give the full picture before diving into each tool: operator catalogue check (quarterly), review scan (monthly), benchmarking snapshot (annual). Say: "We'll go through each one briefly. None of them requires new budget. The most time-intensive takes two hours per quarter."

Slide 8 — Operator catalogue check

Making the spreadsheet feel manageable

The key sell on this slide is that it's the same operators each quarter — you're not starting fresh. You build a short list of 10 operators who sell The Gambia, then visit the same pages four times a year and note what changed.

  • Ask: "Does anyone know which operators currently sell The Gambia to European markets?" Take 2–3 answers. These are the operators who belong on the list.
  • The shared spreadsheet point is important — if GT Board and NCAC each check 5 operators, that's 10 with no duplication. The task naturally splits.
  • If someone asks "which operators?" — D1 and D2 reference Explore Worldwide and Naturetrek. "Those are two you already know. Ask GT Board's international trade contacts for the rest — they almost certainly have a list."
Slide 9 — Review sentiment scan

The methodology pitch — same as Day 1, now owned by the room

The TripAdvisor screenshot on this slide is the same data participants saw being produced in Block 2 yesterday. Make the connection explicit: "Yesterday we showed you what this looks like as a research method. Today we're talking about making it a routine."

  • The four-things framing is the key: themes, tone, complaints, language. Not 20 readings — four observations from 20 reviews. That's the discipline.
  • Who does it: any institution with a legitimate interest in visitor sentiment — GT Board and NCAC are the obvious candidates. Associations can do their sector equivalent (e.g. the Music Union tracks reviews mentioning live music in The Gambia).
  • If time allows: open TripAdvisor on screen briefly. Confirm the Gambia destination page exists, has recent reviews, and takes about 30 minutes to scan. The point is that the data is real and accessible — not a research abstraction.
Slide 10 — Annual benchmarking snapshot

The D1 baseline as a gift

This is a quick slide. The key point is one sentence: the D1 assessment already completed Year 1. They don't need to design a benchmarking framework from scratch — they just need to repeat the same dimensions next October and compare.

  • Say: "Usually benchmarking frameworks are expensive to design and hard to start because you have no baseline. You already have your baseline. It's sitting in D1 and D2. Next year's work is a comparison, not a fresh start."
  • Who does the annual snapshot: most naturally GT Board as lead digital coordinator. But NCAC or an external partner could be commissioned to support it.
Slide 11 — Crisis and reputation management

Agreeing the protocol before you need it

This slide introduces crisis management not as a response to a current crisis, but as a planning tool. The central point is the trigger-window-tone structure: agreed in advance, not improvised during a problem.

  • The Google Alerts screenshot on the right shows monitoring in action — this is what surfaces a crisis signal early. Connect it: "An alert fires. A review appears. The question isn't what you do then — it's whether you have already agreed what to do."
  • The response window ("within 48 hours") is the operational core. Say it plainly: "Every day a negative review sits unanswered, it compounds. The response doesn't need to resolve the problem — it needs to acknowledge it and show it's being taken seriously."
  • For GT Board specifically: "The most important crisis scenario is a cluster of reviews describing similar problems. That's a signal, not an outlier. The quarterly coordination call is where you share those patterns."
  • Tone framing — never defensive — is important. If a participant asks "what if the review is unfair?" acknowledge: "Unfair reviews still need a response. The audience is future visitors reading the exchange, not the reviewer."

Watch for: Participants who raise specific recent incidents (a bad review of a specific operator or attraction). Don't get drawn into the specific case. Keep this at the institutional level: "That's exactly the situation where a pre-agreed protocol removes the pressure of improvising in the moment."

Slide 12 — Platform governance / Who owns what?

The audit question — organisational, not technical

The visitthegambia.com screenshot on the right is an illustration of a real platform that institutions in this room are responsible for. Frame it constructively: "This is an asset. The question is who is responsible for keeping it current, and what the process is when that person changes roles."

  • The "digital orphans" framing is important but potentially sensitive — don't dwell on blame. The D1 baseline identified this as a systemic gap across institutions: projects end, people move on, platforms persist without owners. That's a structural issue, not an individual failure.
  • The platform audit action is simple and non-threatening: "One spreadsheet. One hour. List every official digital touchpoint, note when it was last updated, identify who has access." Frame it as hygiene, not an investigation.
  • Ask the room directly: "For your institution — who has admin access to your official platforms? If that person was not in this room today, would someone else know?" Let the quiet do the work. This is a question they need to ask themselves, not answer out loud.

Watch for: GT Board participants becoming defensive about visitthegambia.com. The website is shown as a real platform, not as an example of a problem. Keep framing forward-looking: "What would ownership and update responsibility look like if you formalised it?"

Slide 13 — The starting KPI set

Don't let this feel like a reporting obligation

Same framing guidance as Day 1 (slide 29 in the Day 1 notes). The four indicators are from D2 Table 20 — they exist already, with 2025 baselines. Participants don't need to design their own framework.

  • Say: "These aren't targets someone else set for you and will hold you to. They're the numbers D2 found. If you track the same four next October, you'll know whether things moved."
  • Note which institution would own which: GT Board owns ITO visibility and TripAdvisor counts; any association can track sentiment for their sector; NCAC can track the overall picture.
  • The pink 2026 Target column is a provocation, not a mandate. "These are reasonable stretch targets based on what's achievable. You can adapt them for your sector."
Slide 14 — From monitoring to SME guidance

Closing the loop to operators

This slide makes the point that monitoring done by institutions and kept inside institutions has limited value. The distribution chain — from monitoring to briefing note to SME action — is what makes the investment worthwhile.

  • The WhatsApp Business catalogue on the right shows a distribution channel SMEs already use. The briefing note doesn't require a new platform — it requires someone to write one page and send it on WhatsApp.
  • Ask: "Who in this room currently communicates monitoring findings to your members or partners?" Take hands. Use whoever raises their hand as a proof point: "What format do you use? How long does it take?" Whatever the answer is, validate it as the model.
  • Tone is everything here. Note the note-box on the slide: "here is what we are seeing and what it might mean for you." Not evaluation. Not instruction. Intelligence sharing between partners.
Slide 15 — The value of monitoring together

A slower slide — let the photo and the proposition breathe

This is a full-bleed photo section with three comparison cards. The visual does the work — don't over-narrate. Read the three comparison points (one institution monitors → three institutions monitor and share → the quarterly call as mechanism) and let the progression land.

  • The conceptual jump here is: monitoring as coordination infrastructure, not just data collection. The quarterly call isn't overhead — it is what transforms individual monitoring into shared intelligence.
  • Ask: "If each of your institutions monitored one element each month and shared findings at a quarterly call — what would the combined picture look like? And what couldn't any one of you see on your own?" This is the bridge to slides 16–17.
Slide 16 — The quarterly coordination call

Making it concrete

Three steps: Share, Identify, Agree. The pink accent on Step 3 (Agree) is intentional — sharing and identifying are interesting; agreeing is where coordination becomes real. Walk through each step and clarify what "agree" means in practice: "Agree: is there one shared action for next quarter? It can be as small as 'GT Board will send a draft operator list to NCAC for review.' Specific. Assigned. Done before the call ends."

  • Duration: "This call should be 90 minutes maximum. If it regularly runs longer, the agenda is too broad. Tighten it."
  • If someone asks "what about quarterly — can't we do it more often?" — "Start quarterly. Monthly is too often to have meaningful new data. Once the routine is established, you can decide whether to adjust frequency."
Slide 17 — Who convenes?

Being direct about roles

The tier-table maps the three tiers in the room to their roles in the coordination mechanism. Read it by row. Don't soften the directness about GT Board's convening role — this is what D2's implementation roadmap recommends, and it reflects GT Board's mandate as destination coordinator.

  • For GT Board: "Convening doesn't mean controlling. It means setting the date, sending the invite, and keeping the shared KPI log. The content comes from everyone."
  • For associations: "Your role as participants is to share what you observe in your sector. Two minutes per institution. Your presence is what makes the shared picture complete."
  • For the Ministry: "Invited, not required for every call. The call works without them. If the Ministry attends, it signals institutional weight. But don't make the call dependent on their attendance."
  • If GT Board pushes back on the convening role: acknowledge the capacity constraint, and note that the mechanism is designed to be light — quarterly, 90 minutes, three steps.

Watch for: If both GT Board and NCAC are present, there may be ambiguity about who leads coordination. Acknowledge it: "D2 recommends GT Board as lead coordinator. That said, if NCAC has more bandwidth, the mechanism works with either. The important thing is that one institution takes ownership."

Slides 18–19 — Digital health check (carousel)
15 min pairs + 5 min share-back

Running the digital health check activity

Slide 18 is the pairs activity. Slide 19 is the share-back. Advance to slide 19 only after pairs have completed the check — keep them on slide 18 while working.

  • Slide 18 setup: "Working in pairs — ideally with someone from a different institution — assess The Gambia's current digital presence across these five dimensions. Circle Strong / Partial / Weak / Unknown. You don't need devices — use your existing knowledge and observations from yesterday."
  • Walk the room. If pairs are stuck on a dimension, prompt them: "Based on what you saw when you searched for your sector yesterday, where would you place that?"
  • Advance to Slide 19 after 15 min. Each pair shares one finding. Facilitator notes on the board in two columns: "Strongest" and "Most critical to fix."
  • The third question on slide 19 — which gap is within your institution's mandate to address — is the most important one. It points directly toward commitments. If time allows, ask pairs to circle one item they could take individual ownership of.

Watch for: Pairs that get stuck in discussion rather than assessment. Keep the tempo: "You'll have a chance to discuss in the share-back. For now, just circle."

Slides 20–21 — Draft your three indicators (carousel)
20 min individual + 10 min pairs + 5 min share-back

Running the indicators activity

This is Block 4's main activity. Distribute the printed indicator sheets before advancing to slide 20. Give clear instructions before participants start writing.

  • Slide 20: "Fill in the sheet individually. Be specific — 'the institution' is not a person. Write a role name or an actual name." Give 20 minutes. Enforce the silence for the first 10 — this is individual work.
  • The four fields are: what to track, how often, who is responsible, how to share. The most important are fields 3 (responsible person) and 4 (sharing channel). Vagueness in either field means the plan won't happen.
  • Advance to Slide 21. Pairs from different institutions exchange sheets and offer one piece of feedback on each of the three criteria on the slide: Is the responsible person specific? Are the indicators checkable? Is the sharing channel realistic?
  • Brief share-back: "What commitments have been made? Where did a pair notice they could support each other's monitoring?" Note any natural coordination pairs — these feed directly into slide 27.
  • If a participant says "I don't know what to track" — direct them to the three monitoring questions (slide 6) and the KPI starting set (slide 13). "Pick one from each source. You have five indicators already."

Watch for: Participants who write monitoring plans that depend on resources or tools they don't currently have. Redirect: "What would you track if you could only use what you have access to right now? Start there. You can expand later."

Block 5 · Commitments and Coordination · Slides 22–30 · 10:45–12:45
Slide 22 — Block 5 divider

Complete gear change

After the divider, say one sentence before advancing: "For the next two hours, I'll be asking more than presenting. The output of this block is specific commitments from every institution and three agreed decisions before we leave the room."

  • Read the sub-text: "Specific. Checkable. Realistic. — and agreed before you leave the room." These are not aspirational — they are the criteria you'll apply throughout the block.
  • If you've noticed participants whose commitments from yesterday were vague, now is the moment to set the expectation: "If your commitment from yesterday said something broad, today is when we make it specific."
Slide 23 — Facilitator synthesis
15 min

The hardest slide in the deck — and the most important

This slide is deliberately mostly empty. You fill it live with 3–4 themes you've observed across the two days. These are your synthesis, not content from the reports. They should reflect what you actually heard — patterns, tensions, recurring concerns, unexpected agreements.

  • Prepare this before the workshop if possible — or during the break. Think: what came up in multiple contexts? What surprised participants? What remained unresolved? Examples: "The monitoring capacity gap is real but the tools are accessible"; "There's appetite for coordination but ambiguity about who convenes"; "The data surprised people — both the gaps and the strengths."
  • Frame each theme without editorialising: "Something I heard repeatedly was..." or "A tension that came up several times was..." — not "you need to fix X."
  • The grounding question on the slide — "What confirmed existing assumptions, and what challenged them?" — is useful if the room is quiet. Both kinds of learning are valuable and worth naming.
  • Give participants 2–3 minutes to sit with the themes before asking for responses. "Anything I've missed? Anything that should be here that isn't?"

Watch for: Participants using this as an opportunity to relitigate Day 1 content or challenge the D1/D2 data. Acknowledge the concern and redirect: "That's worth noting. The question for today is: given what the data shows, what does your institution choose to do?" Synthesis is not debate.

Slide 24 — "One thing that stuck"
10 min

Round-table reflection — keep it moving

Read the prompt aloud: "In one sentence: the finding or idea that was most useful or most surprising." Go round the table. One sentence per person, no extended discussion. Your job is to keep the tempo and note any theme that hasn't appeared in your synthesis yet.

  • If someone gives a long answer, thank them and say: "Hold that thought for the commitment discussion — we'll have space for it shortly."
  • If someone says "nothing surprised me" or "it confirmed what I already knew" — acknowledge: "That's also useful. Confirmation builds confidence to act." Don't push for an insight they don't have.
  • Listen for responses that imply a specific commitment — "I was most surprised by how easy Google Alerts is" = that person should have Google Alerts on their 30-day commitment. Note it.
Slide 25 — One concrete action in 30 days
15 min individual + share-back

The specificity discipline

Give participants 5 minutes to revisit and if needed revise their Day 1 commitment. The criteria on the slide — Specific, Checkable, Realistic — are the filter. Read the example aloud before they write: "Not 'improve our digital presence' — but 'conduct a platform audit and identify three outdated pages to update by June.'"

  • The example sets the standard. If a participant's revised commitment doesn't reach this level of specificity, it's fine to ask once: "Can you give me the version of this that has a date and a specific output?"
  • After individual writing, ask each institution to read their commitment aloud. One sentence. You note them — these are the inputs for slide 26.
  • Don't push back on every vague commitment in the read-around — you'll lose momentum. Make notes and raise specific ones in the amplification discussion (slide 27) or before-leaving discussion (slide 30) if needed.

Watch for: Overly ambitious commitments — "launch a new website," "redesign our TripAdvisor listing," "create a sector digital strategy." Redirect gently: "That's a project. What's the first step you can do in 30 days? That first step is your commitment."

Slide 26 — Commitments board
15 min — facilitator-led grouping

Building the board live

The slide shows four category columns. As participants read their commitments, write or post them under the relevant category. The same four categories as yesterday — Monitor, Make findable, Open a door, Share + coordinate — so participants can see how today's refined commitments compare to yesterday's cards.

  • If a commitment spans two categories, place it in the most primary one. Don't agonise over categorisation — the board is a visual tool, not a taxonomy.
  • After the board is populated, read back by category: "Under Monitor, we have [n] commitments from [institutions]. Three of them are similar — would those three institutions be willing to share what they find with each other?" This is how coordination surfaces organically.
  • Note: any institution with commitments in "Share + coordinate" is already part of the minimum viable mechanism conversation (slide 28). Flag this to the room.
Slide 27 — Where does coordination amplify?
15 min discussion

Finding the amplification pairs

Look at the commitment board. Ask the question on the slide: "Where would two institutions doing something together produce more than two doing it separately?" The three discussion prompts on the slide are starting points — the room should generate specific pairs based on what's actually on the board.

  • Push for names: "Who specifically? GT Board and who? Name the two institutions." Abstraction kills coordination — the pairs need to be specific and mutually acknowledged.
  • The multiplier mapping sheets on the wall (from Block 3 yesterday) should also be visible. Reference them: "Look at what associations said they'd immediately distribute to members if GT Board gave it to them. Is that on the board today? If it is, that's already a coordination pair."
  • Two or three specific amplification pairs is sufficient. Don't try to map the entire coordination landscape — you're looking for the two or three most natural and immediately achievable.
  • If the discussion stalls: read the three prompts on the slide as if they were proposals, not examples. "Would GT Board and NCAC be willing to run a shared quarterly operator check? Who would take that on?"

Watch for: Amplification pairs that require a lot of setup before they can work — shared databases, formal agreements, new platforms. Redirect: "That's a longer-term goal. What can those two institutions share informally, starting with the first quarterly call?"

Slide 28 — A minimum viable mechanism

Don't let it stay abstract

The three steps — Quarterly check-in, Share observations, Agree joint responses — are simple but need anchoring to become real. The pink accent on Step 1 is intentional: "Start here. Nothing more required." This slide should not feel like a proposal for future discussion. It should feel like the agreement you're making right now.

  • Name the step-one attendees out loud: "GT Board, NCAC, and whichever associations want to participate. Who in this room would commit to attending a 90-minute quarterly call?" Ask for raised hands.
  • Note the note-box: "whatever is most likely to actually happen." This is important — the format is secondary to the commitment to meet. A WhatsApp group is legitimate if that's what will happen.
  • If someone says "we already have a coordination meeting" — excellent. "Does it include monitoring data sharing? If not, adding 15 minutes to an existing meeting achieves everything this mechanism requires."
Slide 29 — If there's appetite for more
10 min discussion

Don't sell it — just show it

This slide shows the optional next step: a quarterly briefing note distributed across institutions and to select SMEs. It is an extension of the minimum viable mechanism, not a prerequisite. Frame it as an option, not an expectation.

  • Read the three discussion questions on the right of the slide. These are designed to surface the realistic barriers — time, skills, political will. Give the room 5–8 minutes to discuss in small groups, then brief share-back.
  • If the room is energised about this idea: "Great — make it a commitment today. Who would write the first one? By when?" Move it to the board.
  • If the room is cautious: "That's fine — it starts with the quarterly call. The briefing note becomes an option after the routine is established."
  • The anchor person question ("is there one person that could serve as the anchor?") is the most practically important. An anchor doesn't need authority — they need consistency. Who in the room would do it?
Slide 30 — Three things to agree before the room empties
15 min — enforce this

Getting decisions made before people can leave

This is the most operationally critical slide. Three decisions: date, convener, format. These must be specific and written down before anyone leaves. Do not skip or soften this slide.

  • Date: "Not 'sometime in Q3.' A specific date. Who has their calendar?" Get a date. Write it on the board. Something like: "Tuesday 15 July, 10:00."
  • Convener: "A specific person, not an institution." Wait for a name. If no one volunteers, ask GT Board directly: "Given your D2 mandate as destination coordinator — would you be willing to convene the first call?" If they say yes, get a name, not a title.
  • Format: "Video call, in-person, or shared document? Which is most likely to actually happen?" Get an answer. Write it down.
  • Once all three are agreed: "Does everyone have these three things in your notes? Date, convener, format. Write them now." Give 30 seconds.
  • If the group can't agree on a date in the room: "Before anyone leaves today, exchange contact details with [the convener] and confirm the date by end of business Friday." This is the minimum acceptable outcome.

Watch for: The meeting-within-a-meeting trap — participants trying to set the full agenda for the quarterly call right now. Stop it: "We'll set the agenda before the first call. Right now we just need date, convener, format. Everything else can be agreed by the convener in advance."

Close · Slides 31–33 · 12:45–13:00
Slide 31 — "Maintain momentum."

The close — let the quote land

Advance to this slide and pause. Read the quote: "Maintain momentum. Don't start something new." Read the sub: "The SMEs you met this week are building their digital presence. The infrastructure they need to connect to the world is what you build next."

  • This closes the loop to the SME context: the participant institutions exist in a relationship with the SMEs they serve. Their digital infrastructure work is not abstract — it connects to real operators who need to be found.
  • After reading: "The most important thing about the next 30 days is not innovation — it's follow-through. The momentum you maintain from this workshop will do more than any new initiative you start."
  • Don't add more content here. The quote is the close. Let it sit for a moment.
Slide 32 — One word round the room
5–7 min

The personal close

Go round the table. One word per person — what are you taking away? No explanation required. Facilitator closes: acknowledge the investment participants have made across both days, and frame next steps around maintaining momentum rather than starting something new.

  • Start the round yourself if needed — one word. Keep the pace consistent. The goal is not the words themselves but the act of closing together as a group.
  • After the round: "Those words are a good picture of what this workshop produced. I'll hold them as a summary."
  • Then: three acknowledgements — the institutions for attending both days, ITC YEP for convening, and one specific positive observation from the two days that's genuine and concrete. Don't be generic.
