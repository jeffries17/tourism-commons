Day 1 Facilitator Notes

Gambia Institutional Capacity Workshop · ITC YEP · Banjul · May 2026
These notes are for the facilitator only — not visible in the participant presentation.

Print this document before the workshop. Keep it on the facilitator table. Do not project it.
Before the workshop begins

Room setup

  • Cards and pens on tables — participants will need these for Three Words and Map the Room
  • A visible wall or board for posting commitment cards (used in Day 1 Close and Friday)
  • Flip chart or whiteboard to capture "current words" and "wish words" — these stay visible
  • Test internet connection on at least 2 devices before the session starts
  • Have TripAdvisor Gambia destination page bookmarked and ready for Block 2 live demo
  • Have google.com/alerts open and ready for live Google Alert setup in Block 2

Know your room

  • 13 institutions across 3 tiers — some participants ARE the sectors being discussed (e.g. Craft Market Federation members = the 10% score in the data)
  • The data may be uncomfortable for some. Frame consistently as "starting point" not "evaluation"
  • The Chef Association and culinary sector have minimal data representation — acknowledge this when sector chart comes up: "Some sectors emerging — culinary tourism is one example where we don't yet have strong baseline data"
Opening · Slides 1–4
Slide 2 — Opening introductions
20 min

How to run the introductions

This is deliberately open and conversational — not a structured exercise. The question ("how do you see your connection to promoting The Gambia?") is designed to surface range: some participants will frame their role as oversight, some as service delivery, some as advocacy. All three framings are valid and you'll hear all three.

  • Don't ask for a long answer. "Name, institution, one sentence" keeps it moving.
  • If someone gives a long answer, thank them and say "we'll hear more from you throughout." Don't cut them off — just don't invite more.
  • Listen for anyone who says "I don't really see a connection to tourism promotion" — those participants are often the most important to engage. Acknowledge the uncertainty: "That's exactly why this workshop includes all three tiers."
  • Note recurring themes on the board: oversight / capacity / content / access. Reference them later in Block 3 when discussing what each tier can do.

Watch for: Associations whose members are directly in the tourism supply chain (Tour Guides, Bird Watchers) may frame their role more narrowly than it is. Gently expand: "When a tourist searches for a guide and finds your member — that IS promotion."

Slide 4 — Map the room
15 min

Purpose and facilitation

The map creates a shared visual that participants can point to throughout both days. It externalises the coordination opportunity — which is easier to discuss when it's on a wall than when it's abstract. The slide shows only 3 grouping names — do not pre-assign institutions. Let each institution decide where they belong and place themselves.

  • Group cards into three columns: Government & Regulatory | Creative Industry Associations | Tourism Operations Associations
  • If an institution doesn't fit cleanly, place it where it fits best and note the ambiguity aloud
  • The map stays visible. Refer back to it explicitly in Block 3 (the door only you can open) and Friday's coordination mechanism discussion

Watch for: Institutions that span tiers (e.g. Hospitality Institute provides training — government function, but directly serves SMEs). Place them where they place themselves. Use ambiguous placements as a teaching moment about cross-tier coordination.

Block 1 · The Evidence · Slides 5–21
Slides 6–7 — Three words (carousel)
5 min total

Why two separate slides and why in this order

Participants must commit to their current-perception words before they can write their wish words. The carousel design enforces this — Slide 6 shows only the first question. Advance to Slide 7 only after everyone has written.

  • Slide 6 (current words): Give 3 minutes. After cards are written, ask 3–4 people to share one or two words. Write them on a flip chart column labelled "Now." Do not comment on them yet.
  • Advance to Slide 7 (wish words): "Now a second question." Give 2 minutes. Same process — 3–4 people share, write on a second flip chart column labelled "Wish."
  • Do NOT analyse or discuss the gap between the two lists. Save that for the Block 1 close (Slide 21). Say: "We'll return to these after we've looked at the data."
  • Both flip chart columns stay visible for the rest of the morning.

Watch for: Participants who say "what they say and what I wish they said — it's the same for me." Acknowledge: "That's a good sign for your sector. Hold that thought." · Also watch for negative current words (unsafe, underdeveloped) — these surface real anxieties that the data will contextualise.

Slide 8 — Two reports / one story (Venn)

The methodology pitch

This slide does double duty: it frames the two reports as connected (supply side + demand side) and it sets up the credibility argument for Block 2 ("you can replicate this"). The second job is actually more important for this workshop.

  • Spend 1–2 minutes on the methodology, not just the findings. Name the sources: "We searched operator websites — the same ones any tourist can Google. We read TripAdvisor reviews — the same page you can open right now on your phone."
  • The intersection of the Venn ("the gap = your opportunity") is the core framing of the entire workshop. Say it slowly. Pause after it.
Slide 17 — Your sector in the data

Framing the scores carefully

Some participants will see their sector scored at 10–11% and feel defensive or discouraged. The framing matters enormously.

  • Say explicitly before showing: "Before I show you these numbers, I want to say what they are — and what they are not. They are the starting point the data gives us. They are not an evaluation of your organisation or your work."
  • Emphasise that Photographers Association at 41% demonstrates the ceiling is achievable from within The Gambia
  • If someone challenges the methodology, acknowledge it: "These scores reflect one approach to measurement. They are indicative, not definitive. What matters is the direction — and across nearly every sector, the direction is the same."
  • The Bird Watchers and Security Guards callouts are important — make sure to read them aloud so those representatives feel seen

Watch for: Craft Market Federation reaction to the 10% score. Have a constructive follow-up ready: "10% also means there's more headroom for improvement here than in any other sector. That's an opportunity, not a judgment."

Slide 19 — Tools are free

The pivot point

This is the most important conceptual moment in Block 1. Participants need to leave this slide believing that what produced D1 and D2 is something they can do themselves. Without that belief, Block 2 loses credibility.

  • Point to the TripAdvisor mockup: "This is the page. You can open it right now." Pause.
  • Point to the Google search mockup: "This is what someone searching for the Craft Market Federation finds. One search. 30 seconds." Pause.
  • Close with: "In the next block, we're not going to talk about monitoring in theory. We're going to do it. You'll set up your first Google Alert before we break for lunch."
Slides 20–21 — Find your sector (carousel)
20 min

Managing the activity

  • Slide 20 (individual search): Give a clear time signal — "You have 5 minutes. Start now." Walk the room. Note what people are finding and not finding.
  • Advance to Slide 21 after 5 min. Pairs discussion (8 min) is followed by share-back (7 min).
  • Capture on the board: "present / visible but not bookable / effectively invisible" — these three categories will reappear in Block 2 (Make your sector findable)
  • The callback to three words: After share-back, turn to the flip chart: "Look at the words from this morning. How does what you just found connect to those lists? The gap between column 1 and column 2 — that gap is what the 9% and the 16.9 score represent."
  • This is the emotional close of Block 1. Give it a moment.

Watch for: Participants who find their organisation easily may feel less urgency. Redirect: "What about your members? Would a Dutch tourist booking a trip find them by name?"

If internet fails

Contingency for Block 1 activities

  • Three words: No internet needed — proceed as normal
  • Find your sector: Use pre-printed screenshots. Prepare 4 searches in advance: "craft market gambia," "live music banjul," "tour guide gambia," "birdwatching gambia." Print one set per table. Groups annotate what they notice.

Block 2 · Things 1 + 2 · Slides 22–34 · 10:45–12:15
Slide 22–23 — Block 2 divider + Not the marketer

Setting the register for the afternoon

The divider slide is a gear change — shorter, quieter. Pause after advancing. Then "Not the marketer" (slide 23) is the conceptual anchor for the whole block. Read the two framing questions aloud: "How do we promote The Gambia?" is the wrong question. "How do we make it easier for others to promote it well?" is the right one.

  • This reframe matters most for GT Board and Ministry participants who may feel personally responsible for the full promotional agenda. Acknowledge that: "This isn't about lowering ambition — it's about locating where your institution has the most leverage."
  • It also matters for associations who may feel this workshop isn't for them. The enabler framing makes it explicit that they ARE the infrastructure — not a passive audience for government strategy.
Slide 24 — Four things overview

Naming all four before going into detail

Spend 2 minutes on this card grid. Give the full picture before diving into Thing 1. The reason: some participants will immediately know which thing is most relevant to them. Naming all four lets them invest in the detail knowing where it's going.

  • Say explicitly: "We'll cover Things 1 and 2 this morning, Things 3 and 4 after lunch. Each one is free or near-free, works within your existing mandate, and takes less than 2 hours a month to do consistently."
  • If someone asks "what about social media / content creation / digital advertising?" — acknowledge and park it: "Those belong in the SME training. Your role is upstream of those choices."
Slide 25 — Thing 1 · Monitor: what the world sees
5 min

The Responsible Travel screenshot makes the absence concrete

This slide shows a real operator page with a real sidebar. Point to it directly: "This is a well-regarded UK operator. Their Gambia destination guide sidebar lists four things: 2-minute summary, best time to visit, river cruise, birdwatching. That's it. No music. No crafts. No festivals. No visual arts."

  • Ask the room: "Is this operator wrong? No — they're reflecting what they can find and sell. The absence is not their failure. It's a signal about what reaches them."
  • For associations: "If a Dutch traveller uses this guide to plan their Gambia trip, your members don't exist in their itinerary."

Watch for: Participants who say "we need to contact this operator." Yes, eventually — that's Thing 3 (open a door). But the monitoring point is prior: you need to know what's on these pages before you can advocate for change. Keep this slide focused on observation, not outreach.

Slide 26 — Four free tools

Keep it concrete and unintimidating

  • Stress "free" on every tool. The common objection is "we don't have budget." Remove that before it's raised.
  • Google Trends is often unfamiliar — demonstrate briefly on screen if time allows. Type "The Gambia tourism" and compare to "Senegal tourism." The gap is visible in under 30 seconds.
  • Don't spend more than 1 minute per tool. The detail comes on the next three slides.
Slide 27 — Google Alert live setup
10 min — live activity

Running the live setup

This is the highest-participation moment in Block 2. Every person in the room should leave having set at least one alert. The screenshot of the live alert (showing GT Board news, Gambia tourism results) is proof that it works — show it before asking participants to set their own.

  • Say clearly before they start: "Take out your phone or laptop. Go to google.com/alerts. I'll give you 5 minutes to set two alerts: 'Gambia tourism' and your sector name + Gambia."
  • Walk the room while they do it. Check screens. If someone can't get the page to load, have them write the two alerts on paper — they can set it when they return to the office.
  • When you come back together: ask 2–3 people what alert they set for their sector. Read them aloud. This names specific sectors and reinforces that every association has a relevant search term.

Watch for: Participants who say "I already have Google Alerts." Good — ask them what they do with the results. That's the more important question, and it sets up the KPI slide that follows.

Slide 28 — TripAdvisor scan demo
8 min

The annotation is the teaching

The TripAdvisor screenshot is annotated with four labels: Natural environment dominates; Craft market appears once; Zero music, arts, or creative references; Safety mentioned positively in 3 reviews. Walk through each label. These map directly to the D2 findings — this is what producing D1 looked like in practice.

  • The point is methodology, not content: "We didn't build a special system to read these reviews. We spent an hour reading them and noting patterns. You can do the same thing — 20 reviews a month, 4 things to look for."
  • Have the actual TripAdvisor Gambia destination page open on a second device. If you have time, show the real page and scroll briefly. Let participants see that the data they saw in Block 1 is this legible.
Slide 29 — KPI tracking table

Don't let this feel like a reporting obligation

The D2 Table 20 KPI set is a gift — it's a ready-made starting framework. The risk is that it looks like a reporting requirement participants will be held to. Frame it differently.

  • Say: "These aren't targets someone else set for you. They're the numbers D2 found in October. If you track the same four indicators in October next year, you'll know whether things moved — without designing a measurement framework from scratch."
  • Note which institution would own which indicator: GT Board owns ITO visibility; any association can track review sentiment for their sector; GT Board/NCAC own the TripAdvisor review count.
  • The "2 hours/month" note is important — repeat it. The ambition here is routine, not rigour.
Slides 30–31 — Thing 2: aggregation gap + before/after
10 min

The blog screenshot lands the point better than theory

Slide 30 shows a real travel blog listing "12 Reasons to Visit The Gambia." Creative arts and culture is item 8 of 12 — and when you get to it, there are no booking links, no directory, no way to act on it. Point to that explicitly: "The desire is there. The infrastructure to fulfil it isn't."

  • The before/after (slide 31) should be quick — 2 minutes. The left side (scattered, inconsistent) is what everyone just experienced in the "find your sector" activity. The right side (maintained directory, WhatsApp catalogue) is achievable immediately.
  • Emphasise: "The right side doesn't need a web developer. It can be a Google Sheet that one person updates once a month." The WhatsApp Business catalogue screenshot makes this real.
Slides 32–33 — What each association can own + VisitTheGambia.com

Be direct about who does what

Slide 32 names specific associations. Read the cards aloud by name. This is one of the few moments where an association sees their institution's name on a slide as an actor, not a beneficiary.

  • For GT Board: the VisitTheGambia homepage screenshot on slide 33 shows what currently exists — "Discover The Smiling Coast." Ask directly: "What would a creative industries section on this site look like? Not a redesign. A new section. Who would maintain it?" This is a live planning question, not rhetorical.
  • For Craft Market Federation: "A member directory with photos and WhatsApp contacts. That is a morning's work to set up, and one person one hour per month to maintain."

Watch for: GT Board participants becoming defensive about the current site. Don't frame as a criticism — frame as an opportunity: "The site has the foundation. What would the creative industries section add to what's already there?"

Slide 34 — Is your member findable? + gap activity
10 min — activity

Running the show of hands

Ask the question directly: "For your institution or association — is there currently one place where someone searching for your sector online would find accurate, current information?" Then: "Show of hands: YES / PARTIALLY / NO."

  • The Google Business Profile screenshot (Gorilla Neighbour Guest House, Uganda) shows what a good single-entry findability solution looks like. It's not glamorous — it's a listing with a photo, phone number, reviews, and a directions button. Ask: "Would your members appear if someone searched for them by name in Google?"
  • After the show of hands, note the split. Don't comment on who said NO — use it as a group signal: "Most sectors don't have one findable place. That's the gap Thing 2 closes."
  • If the discussion runs long, cut to: "Write YES / PARTIALLY / NO on your notepad with one word for what's missing. We'll come back to this in Friday's commitments."
Lunch · 12:15–13:15

Between Block 2 and Block 3

  • Check who hasn't yet set a Google Alert — remind at the end of lunch to do it on their phone
  • Note from the show of hands: which associations said PARTIALLY or NO. Reference by name in Block 3 when discussing what each tier can do.
  • The afternoon is more discussion-heavy. Set the tone coming back: "This afternoon we move from tools to relationships."
Block 3 · Things 3 + 4 · Slides 35–43 · 13:15–14:45
Slide 35–36 — Block 3 divider + The door only you can open

The shift from tools to relationships

Things 1 and 2 were about systems — alerts, directories, routines. Things 3 and 4 are about credibility and relationships, which can't be automated. The opening of Block 3 should feel different — slower, more personal.

  • Slide 36's question ("What is the one door only you can open?") is rhetorical at this point — you're not asking participants to answer it yet. You're setting up the activity that comes in two slides. Let the question land first.
  • The three types of door-opening (buyers, audiences, platforms) are the structure for the activity. Read them aloud but don't over-explain — the examples will do the work.
Slide 37 — What each tier can do

Making it concrete without prescribing

This table maps specific actions to the three tiers in the room. Walk through it by row. The goal is recognition, not instruction — participants should see themselves in the row and nod.

  • For GT Board / Ministry row: "Operator fam trips and trade fair delegations — these are within your existing remit. The question isn't whether you can do this, it's whether you're doing it with creative industry SMEs in the delegation, not just hotel and resort partners."
  • For associations row: "Member recommendations to operators. If a tour operator is looking for a crafts experience and calls GT Board, GT Board can only refer them to your directory. If your directory doesn't exist, the referral ends there."
  • Point back to the wall map from the morning: "Every row in this table connects to a column on that map. That's the coordination opportunity."
Slide 38 — Activity: The one door (10 min)
10 min

Running the activity

Each participant writes one specific introduction, invitation, or access they could broker in the next six months. Pairs share, then 4–5 read to the group. Group them on the board by type: Operator access · Journalist/press · Platform/portal · Trade fair.

  • The key word is "specific." If someone writes "improve relationships with operators," push: "Which operator? What introduction?" Specificity is the difference between a wish and a plan.
  • If a participant says "I don't have those kinds of relationships," redirect: "What door can you open for your members within your own network? A peer referral, a direct introduction at a sector meeting, recommending a member to NCAC for a press trip?"
  • Keep this at 10 minutes — the multiplier mapping activity (slide 42) is longer and more important. Don't let this expand.

Watch for: Security Guard Association and Chef Association may feel this activity doesn't apply to them — "we're not the ones opening doors to operators." Reframe: The Security Guard Association's relationship with GT Board IS the door — they can advocate for their members to be cited in safety perception campaigns. Chefs can propose to GT Board a culinary tourism pilot to be included in the next fam trip.

Slides 39–40 — Thing 4: Briefing intro + what a good briefing looks like

The blog screenshot does double duty

Slide 39 shows the same travel blog from Block 2 — "Is The Gambia Worth Visiting?" — but now from a different angle. Block 2 used it to illustrate the aggregation gap (no booking links). Block 3 uses it to illustrate what a member briefing could reference: "Travellers are writing about Gambian art and culture as a reason to visit. Does your Music Union know that? Does your Craft Market Federation know that?"

  • The point: monitoring produces intelligence that members don't know to ask for. Translating that into a quarterly message is Thing 4.

Slide 40's three-card layout (What we saw / What it means for you / One thing to do) is a template, not a prescription. Read the Music Union example in the briefing mock as if you're reading an actual briefing. It should take 60 seconds — that's how long a good briefing takes to read.

  • After reading it: "Does that feel useful if you're a musician trying to build your international profile? Yes. Does it require the Music Union to produce a 12-page report? No."
  • Stress: "Whatever your members actually read." Not email if they don't open email. Not a PDF if they're on WhatsApp all day.
Slide 41 — From monitoring to briefing (3-step)

Closing the loop

This is a connection slide — showing how Thing 1 (monitoring) produces the inputs for Thing 4 (briefing). It's the shortest in Block 3. Don't over-explain it. Read the three steps aloud and move on.

  • The pink accent on Step 3 is intentional — the value is in the sharing, not the monitoring itself. Monitoring that never gets shared is worthless to members.
Slide 42 — Association multiplier mapping activity
20 min

The most important activity of Block 3

This is the moment where associations stop being observers and become sources of intelligence for GT Board and NCAC. It's structured so that the room generates the coordination opportunities themselves — you don't need to prescribe them.

  • Setup: Have printed A5 sheets ready with the three questions. Each participant fills in their sheet individually (5 min), then groups of three share (8 min). Then each group posts their sheets on a wall section.
  • Reading back: Walk to the wall. Read aloud what associations said they would immediately distribute to members. The list will contain things like "a list of bookable experiences," "information about the fam trip," "tips for setting up a TripAdvisor listing." These are the coordination outputs — things GT Board and NCAC could produce once and associations would distribute to hundreds of people.
  • Say explicitly at the end: "Everything on that wall is a potential agenda item for Friday's coordination conversation. We'll come back to it."

Watch for: The third question — "one thing GT Board or NCAC could give you that you'd immediately distribute" — may produce unexpected answers. Some will be content (marketing materials, briefing notes). Some will be access (invitations, referrals). Some will be training. Record all of them. They reveal what associations actually need, which is often different from what oversight bodies assume they need.

Slide 43 — The multiplier logic

Closing Block 3

The hub-and-spoke diagram makes the coordination argument visual. The point is simple: one piece of content produced by GT Board, distributed through 9 associations, reaches hundreds of businesses. That's not a bureaucratic structure — it's a distribution advantage.

  • Read the two examples aloud: the quarterly briefing example (1 → 9 → hundreds) and the events calendar example (1 calendar → 3 associations → 3 audiences).
  • Close with: "The network for this coordination already exists in this room. The only question is whether we use it."
  • Then advance to the break without further comment. Let the visual sit.

Day 1 Close · Slides 44–47 · 15:00–16:00
Slide 44 — Day 1 Close divider

Setting up the close

This is the only section with no new content. It's a gear change: slower pace, more personal register. After the divider slide, say one sentence before advancing: "We've covered a lot of ground. The next hour is about what it means for you specifically — not for institutions in general."

Slide 45 — The journalist test
25 min (or 15 min if running late)

Why this activity and why now

The journalist test is the emotional close of Day 1. It personalises everything — the D2 data, the four tools, the multiplier logic — into one concrete scenario. Every participant should feel it is about them, not about institutional strategy in the abstract.

  • Read the scenario aloud before participants write. Don't put it up and let people read at their own pace — read it as if you're describing a real situation: "A journalist from a Dutch travel magazine is writing a piece on cultural tourism in The Gambia. They search online for contacts, experiences, and bookable products. Would they find your organisation? Would they find your members?"
  • Give 8 minutes for individual writing. Enforce the silence — this is the one moment of the day where participants are writing for themselves, not for the group.
  • Pairs: 8 minutes. Brief share-back: ask 4–5 people for one finding. Capture on the board in three columns: "Currently findable" / "Visible but not bookable" / "Effectively invisible."
  • The three columns connect directly to Friday — these are the baseline categories that commitments should shift. Note which institutions end up in each column.

Watch for: Participants who say "we're visible but only in X language" — this is an important nuance, especially given the D2 finding about the near-total absence of French content. Note it on the board. It connects to the source market data from Block 1 (15% of reviews are Francophone, highest unmet demand).

Why Dutch? If asked — "Dutch/Belgian visitors are The Gambia's most satisfied source market (D2 §4.4 — average 4.56/5). If we can't be found by them, we're leaving the easiest conversion on the table."

Slide 46 — 30-day commitments
20 min

Making commitments real

This is the output of Day 1. Not the four tools, not the data, not the discussion — the commitments. Give the instruction clearly before anyone writes: "One thing. Thirty days. Specific enough that you'd know whether it happened."

  • Give examples of what specific looks like: "Set up a Google Alert for my sector by end of next week" — good. "Improve our digital presence" — too vague, push it back.
  • Give participants 5 minutes to write. Then ask each institution to read their commitment aloud. One sentence. No explanation needed unless they want to add one.
  • As they read, post cards on the board under the four categories (Monitor / Make findable / Open a door / Share + coordinate). Read back the board by category at the end.
  • Note where multiple institutions have similar commitments — say those explicitly: "Three institutions have committed to setting up a Google Alert. Can they share what they find? That's already the beginning of a coordination habit."
  • These cards stay on the board. They are the input for Friday's Block 5 commitments session.

Watch for: Overly ambitious commitments — "launch a new website by end of month." Gently redirect: "What's the first step toward that? What can you specifically do in 30 days?" The goal is a kept promise, not an impressive-sounding aspiration.

Slide 47 — See you tomorrow

Closing the day

Keep this brief. Two things to say: (1) what Friday covers ("how you know it's working, and what you agree to do together"), and (2) the optional overnight task ("search for your sector one more time tonight and write one sentence: what would it take to change one result?").

  • Don't run a reflection discussion here — the commitments activity was the close. End on time.
  • If the commitment cards board is well-populated, acknowledge it: "There are [n] commitments on that board. Friday, we'll build on them."
